# Roblox, October 2021

> **What happened:** A 73-hour outage. A newly enabled feature on the Consul cluster — used for service discovery across the whole platform — caused contention that degraded the cluster, and a latent performance bug in its storage layer prevented recovery. Diagnosis was slow because the observability systems depended on Consul too.
>
> *Source: Roblox's published incident analysis, January 2022.*

<C color="orange">The longest and most instructive of these outages</C>, because almost nothing about it was a hardware failure — it was a single foundational dependency, a change with unforeseen interaction, and recovery paths that all led back through the thing that was broken.

<Plain>

A large campus runs on one central directory. Every building consults it to find out where anything is — who provides what service, which room, which floor.

The directory works well and everything depends on it, which nobody thinks about because it has always worked.

A new feature is switched on: the directory will now **push updates** to anyone who asks, rather than waiting to be asked. Useful, and it means the directory is doing considerably more work than before.

Under real load, the extra work causes contention. The directory slows. <C color="crimson">And because everything consults it constantly, everything slows with it.</C>

Now the compounding part.

**Diagnosis requires the directory.** The monitoring systems look up where things are — through the directory. So as it degrades, visibility degrades with it, exactly when it is most needed.

**Restarting does not help**, because a subtle problem in how the directory writes its records makes it slow to come back and slow to stay healthy under load — and that behaviour only appears at this size, which is why it was never seen in testing.

**And there is nowhere to route around.** Every building needs the directory. There is no path that avoids it.

<H>Three days later it is fixed. Not because the failure was complex, but because every route to understanding and repairing it ran through the thing that had failed.</H>

</Plain>

## 2. What transfers

<Depth title="Foundational dependencies, and the changes that are riskier than they look">

**1. Some dependencies have no degraded mode, and they deserve different treatment.** Most components can [degrade](../10-reliability/03-graceful-degradation.md) — serve stale data, hide a section, fall back to a default. <C color="crimson">Service discovery, DNS, authentication and configuration cannot</C>: there is no meaningful "partly working" state for *"where is the thing I need to call?"*

For these, the usual reliability toolkit does not apply, and different measures are needed:

- **Cache aggressively at the client**, with long TTLs, so a brief coordination outage does not immediately stop everything. A service that remembers where its dependencies were five minutes ago survives a five-minute discovery outage.
- **Fail static rather than fail closed** — if configuration cannot be fetched, keep using the last known good values indefinitely rather than erroring.
- **Partition the dependency** so one cluster's failure does not affect everything — separate coordination clusters per region or per major subsystem.
- **Change it with far more caution than its apparent risk suggests**, because its blast radius is total.

**2. Blast radius should be assessed by dependents, not by change size.** Enabling a feature flag on a coordination cluster is a small change with an enormous blast radius. <C color="orange">The risk of a change is a property of what depends on the component, not of how many lines were altered.</C>

A practical rule: components on which everything depends should receive the **most** staged rollouts, the longest soak times, and the most explicit rollback plans — which is usually the opposite of how they are treated, because they are infrastructure rather than product and their changes look small.

**3. Some behaviours only appear at production scale.** The contention and the storage bug were both load- and size-dependent. <C color="crimson">No staging environment reproduces production's data volume, traffic mix and concurrency simultaneously</C>, which is a limit on testing rather than a failure of it.

The responses are: **stage rollouts in production** so the first exposure is small; **soak** at each stage long enough for load-dependent effects to appear; and treat "it worked in staging" as **weak** evidence for infrastructure changes.

**4. Observability must not share a failure domain with the platform.** This is the fourth postmortem in a row where it mattered, and here it was most costly. <C color="green">Telemetry, dashboards, alerting and runbook access should run on separate infrastructure</C> — a different cluster, ideally a different provider — precisely so they are available when the primary platform is not.

**5. Recovery from total failure requires gradual re-entry.** A recovered coordination cluster receiving the whole platform's reconnection attempts at once fails again immediately — every client retrying, every cache cold, every connection re-establishing simultaneously. <C color="green">Recovery plans need a documented staged re-entry</C>: bring back a fraction of traffic, confirm stability, expand.

This is the [thundering herd](../07-caching/04-cache-failure-modes.md) at platform scale, and it is why "the fix is deployed" is not the same as "the incident is over".

**The overall reading.** Three days of downtime for a change that looked routine, on a component that was working perfectly, discovered slowly because the tools for discovery were downstream of the problem.

<H>The single most transferable question: for each foundational component, what happens if it is unavailable for an hour — and can we see, diagnose and recover without it? If the honest answer is no, that is the highest-value reliability work available, and it is almost never the work that gets prioritised.</H>

</Depth>

## 3. The pattern across all four postmortems

| Incident | Trigger | What made it long |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **[S3 2017](./07-aws-s3-2017.md)** | Operator input error | Unexercised restart path; dashboard depended on S3 |
| **[GitHub 2018](./08-github-2018.md)** | 43-second partition | Data divergence cannot be fixed by restarting |
| **[Cloudflare 2019](./09-cloudflare-2019.md)** | One regex | Global instant deployment; remediation path affected |
| **Roblox 2021** | A feature flag | No degraded mode; observability downstream; latent bug blocked restart |

<C color="orange">In every case the trigger was small and the duration was determined by something else entirely:</C> an untested recovery path, a class of damage that cannot be waited out, a deployment model with no staging, or a dependency with no way around it.

<H>The trigger is unique to each incident and unpredictable. The amplifiers are the same four every time — and they are the ones you can work on in advance.</H>

## Rapid-fire recall

1. What triggered the incident, and why was the change considered low risk?
2. Why is service discovery unable to degrade gracefully?
3. Give four measures appropriate for a dependency with no degraded mode.
4. Why did restarting not resolve it?
5. Why did diagnosis take so long?
6. How should the blast radius of a change be assessed?
7. Why is "it worked in staging" weak evidence for infrastructure changes?
8. Why must re-entry after a total outage be gradual?
9. What single question should be asked of every foundational component?
10. Across all four postmortems, what varies and what stays the same?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Enabling a streaming feature on the Consul cluster** used for service discovery. It looked low risk because it was a **small configuration change intended as an improvement** — the risk lay in what depended on the component, not in the change itself.
2. Because there is **no meaningful partly-working state for "where is the service I need to call?"** Unlike a recommendations panel, it cannot serve a stale or default answer indefinitely and still function.
3. **Aggressive client-side caching with long TTLs** · **fail static** (keep last known good values rather than erroring) · **partition the dependency** into separate clusters per region or subsystem · **change it with far more caution** than its size suggests.
4. Because a **latent performance bug in the underlying storage layer** meant the cluster could not return to health at that data size and load simply by restarting — removing the standard first remediation.
5. Because the **observability stack itself depended on Consul**, so visibility degraded exactly as the incident deepened, making hypotheses hard to test.
6. **By what depends on the component, not by the size of the change.** A one-line flag on a coordination cluster has total blast radius; a large refactor of a leaf service has almost none.
7. Because **contention, hot spots and storage-engine behaviour are load- and size-dependent**, and no staging environment reproduces production's data volume, traffic mix and concurrency together.
8. Because every client is retrying, every cache is cold and every connection is re-establishing simultaneously — **restoring full load to a just-recovered system repeats the failure**. It is the thundering herd at platform scale.
9. <H>If this is unavailable for an hour, can we still see, diagnose and recover without it?</H> If not, that is the highest-value reliability work available.
10. **The trigger varies and is unpredictable** — a typo, a partition, a regex, a flag. **The amplifiers are the same four**: unexercised recovery paths, damage that cannot be waited out, deployment with no staging, and dependencies with no way around them.

</details>
