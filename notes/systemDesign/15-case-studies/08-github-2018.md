# GitHub, October 2018

> **What happened:** A 43-second network partition between two data centres, during planned maintenance, triggered an automated database failover. Both sides briefly accepted writes. Reconciling the divergence took over 24 hours of degraded service.
>
> *Source: GitHub's published incident analysis, October 2018.*

<C color="orange">The clearest available demonstration that recovery time is unrelated to failure duration</C> — and that automation which is correct in the common case can be catastrophic in a rare one.

<Plain>

A company keeps its records in two offices, East and West. East holds the master copy; West keeps a continuously updated duplicate.

A rule exists for safety: if West cannot reach East for more than a few seconds, West assumes East is gone and **promotes itself to master**. Sensible — it means a genuine disaster does not stop the business.

One afternoon a cable is being replaced and the link drops for **43 seconds**.

East is completely fine. It has not noticed anything and keeps accepting new records.

West, unable to reach East, follows the rule and promotes itself. <C color="crimson">Now both offices are accepting records and each believes it is the master.</C>

The link comes back after 43 seconds and the problem becomes visible. Both offices have records the other does not, some of them concerning the same things. There is no rule for merging them — the system was designed on the assumption that only one master exists.

<H>The outage lasted 43 seconds. Working out which records were real, reconciling them, and restoring confidence took more than a day.</H>

And the reason is uncomfortable: the automation did **exactly what it was told**, correctly, in a situation its designers had not distinguished from the one it was built for.

</Plain>

## 2. What transfers

<Depth title="Failover automation, and why the old primary is the problem">

**The core difficulty is undecidable locally.** A replica that cannot reach the primary has two possible worlds:

- **The primary is dead.** Promote immediately — every second of delay is downtime.
- **The network is partitioned and the primary is fine.** Do **not** promote — doing so creates two primaries.

<C color="crimson">These are indistinguishable from the replica's position, and they demand opposite actions.</C> No amount of cleverness in the replica resolves it, because the information required is on the other side of the partition.

**The mechanisms that actually help:**

**1. A majority quorum decides, not the replica.** With an odd number of voting nodes across three failure domains, promotion requires a **majority**. A minority partition **cannot** promote itself, because it cannot reach a quorum — which structurally prevents two primaries. <C color="green">This is why consensus-based failover (Raft, or an external quorum service) is preferred over pairwise heartbeats.</C>

**2. Fencing the old primary.** Promotion should make the old primary's writes **impossible**, not merely unwanted. A [fencing token](../06-distributed-systems/03-consensus-and-quorums.md) — a monotonically increasing epoch that storage checks and rejects if stale — means a deposed primary's writes are refused even if it never learns it was deposed. <C color="green">Without fencing, failover relies on the old primary noticing, which it may not.</C>

**3. Tuning the timeout is a real trade with no correct answer.** Too short and a transient blip triggers an unnecessary failover — itself disruptive, and the direct cause here. Too long and genuine failures extend downtime. <C color="orange">A 43-second threshold is short enough that ordinary maintenance can cross it</C>, which argues for longer thresholds where the cost of downtime is lower than the cost of divergence.

**4. Consider requiring human confirmation for cross-region promotion.** Automated failover **within** a region, where partitions are rarer and blast radius smaller, is usually right. Automated **cross-region** promotion is where split brain becomes most likely and most damaging. Many organisations deliberately keep a human in that loop, accepting minutes of downtime to avoid a day of reconciliation.

**The larger lessons:**

<H>Recovery time is unrelated to failure duration. It is determined by what kind of damage occurred — and damage to data consistency is the slowest kind to repair, because it cannot be fixed by restarting anything.</H>

That reframes what "availability" work should target. Reducing a 43-second outage to 10 seconds would have changed nothing here; **preventing divergence** would have changed everything.

**Automation amplifies whatever it encodes.** The failover system worked precisely as designed, and its design did not distinguish a dead primary from an unreachable one. <C color="orange">Automation that acts correctly in the common case and catastrophically in a rare one is more dangerous than no automation</C>, because it acts faster than anyone can intervene and is trusted by default.

The practical response is to enumerate, for any automated remediation: *what does this do if its input is wrong?* and *what is the worst outcome it can produce unsupervised?* Then bound that outcome — the same [guardrail principle](./07-aws-s3-2017.md) as the S3 tooling.

**A note on the response itself, which was good.** GitHub chose **degraded service over inconsistent data** and communicated openly for the duration. For a system of record that is the correct trade, and it is worth noticing that this was a deliberate CAP decision made under pressure — the kind that is much easier to make well when the principle was agreed in advance.

</Depth>
