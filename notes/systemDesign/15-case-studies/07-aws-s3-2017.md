# AWS S3, February 2017

> **What happened:** During debugging of a billing system issue, an authorised operator ran a routine command with an incorrect input, removing far more capacity than intended. Two S3 subsystems in `us-east-1` had to be fully restarted — something that had not been done in years — and the restart took hours.
>
> *Source: AWS's published service summary, 28 February 2017.*

<C color="orange">The canonical outage for three lessons</C>: blast radius on operator tooling, restart paths that are never exercised, and monitoring that depends on the thing being monitored.

<Plain>

An engineer is fixing a billing problem in a warehouse and needs to take a few shelving units offline.

They run the standard procedure and mistype the quantity. <C color="crimson">Instead of a few units, a large section is removed.</C>

The procedure did exactly what it was told. It did not ask *"are you sure — that is most of the building?"*, because it had no notion of how much removal was too much.

Two critical systems lose enough capacity to stop working: the index that says where everything is, and the system that decides where new things go. Without the index, nothing can be found even though every item is physically present and undamaged.

Then the second problem, which turns a mistake into an outage. <C color="crimson">These systems have not been fully restarted in years.</C> They have grown enormously in that time, and starting them means rebuilding the index from scratch — a process nobody has timed at current size, because nobody has had to do it.

It takes hours.

And the third: the board in the lobby showing whether the warehouse is operating **is itself hosted in the warehouse.** So during the outage it cheerfully reports that everything is fine, because it cannot reach the system that would tell it otherwise.

</Plain>

## 2. What transfers

<Depth title="Guardrails, exercised recovery, and the independence of your monitoring">

**1. Powerful tooling needs bounds, not just authorisation.** The operator was authorised and the command was correct in form. <C color="crimson">What was missing was a limit on *how much* it could do.</C>

Practical guardrails, in rough order of value:

- **Cap the blast radius per invocation** — a tool may remove at most N% of capacity, and larger operations require an explicit override.
- **Require confirmation proportional to impact** — typing a number is fine for one host and should not be for a hundred.
- **Refuse to take a subsystem below its minimum viable capacity** — the tool should know that threshold and enforce it.
- **Prefer gradual application** — remove capacity in stages with health checks between, so the effect is observed before it is complete.

<C color="green">The general principle: a tool that can cause an outage should make causing one require deliberate effort.</C> AWS's own follow-up included exactly this — capacity-removal limits and a floor below which the tooling refuses to go.

**2. Recovery paths decay unless exercised.** The restart worked; it simply took far longer than anyone expected, because the system had grown by orders of magnitude since it was last performed.

<H>An untested recovery path has an unknown duration, and an unknown duration means your RTO is fiction.</H>

This is the same finding as [untested backups](../10-reliability/04-disaster-recovery.md): the mechanism exists, and nobody knows what it costs. The counter-practice is deliberate exercise — restart components on a schedule, in production, at current scale, and **measure how long it takes**. If that is too frightening to do deliberately, it is far too frightening to discover during an incident.

A second-order point: the fix is often to make recovery **incremental** — partitioning state so a restart can proceed in independent chunks, rather than requiring the whole subsystem to come up at once. AWS's follow-up work included exactly this kind of partitioning.

**3. Monitoring must not depend on what it monitors.** The status dashboard failing is the detail everyone remembers, and it generalises further than people apply it:

| Dependency | Failure |
| :--- | :--- |
| Status page hosted on the affected infrastructure | Cannot report the outage |
| Alerting pipeline through the failing message broker | Alerts never fire |
| Dashboards querying the down database | No visibility during the incident |
| Runbooks in a wiki behind the failing SSO | Cannot read the recovery steps |
| On-call paging via a service on the same cloud region | Nobody is woken |

<C color="green">The rule: your ability to observe and respond must have a **different failure domain** from the thing being observed.</C> Separate account, separate provider, separate region — and, for the most critical paths, verified by deliberately cutting the dependency and confirming you can still see and act.

**4. Foundational dependencies concentrate risk invisibly.** S3's unavailability affected services whose owners had never listed S3 as a dependency — it was underneath something else they used. <C color="orange">Dependency graphs are deeper than teams believe</C>, and the practical response is to ask, for each critical path, *"what does this transitively depend on, and what happens if that is unavailable?"* — then to design a degraded mode for the answers.

</Depth>
